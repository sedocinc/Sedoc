Quotation vs Invoice: What's the Difference?

Two documents that look similar but serve completely different purposes in a client relationship.

sedoc Team · Updated September 2026 · Quotation Maker

The core difference

A quotation is an estimate you send before work begins — it proposes a price for the client to review and approve. An invoice is a request for payment sent after (or during) the work, based on what was actually agreed or delivered. A quotation is a proposal; an invoice is a demand for payment.

Why mixing them up causes real problems

Sending a document labeled "Invoice" when you actually mean "here's my estimate" can create the impression that payment is due immediately, which damages trust with a new client. Conversely, sending a "Quotation" after work is already complete makes it look like you haven't formally billed them at all, which can delay payment because it doesn't read as an official request.

What a good quotation includes

  • An estimated cost breakdown by item or service, similar to an invoice's line items
  • A validity period — "This quote is valid for 30 days" — since prices for materials or time can change
  • Clear terms about what happens if scope changes after acceptance
  • A space for the client to formally approve or sign off, converting it into an agreement

The typical workflow

  1. Quotation sent — you propose the price and scope.
  2. Client approves — verbally, by signature, or by email confirmation.
  3. Work is completed (or delivery milestones are reached).
  4. Invoice sent — based on the approved quotation, now formally requesting payment.

Keeping the quotation number referenced on the final invoice ("Ref: Quotation #Q-2026-014") makes it easy for both sides to trace the final bill back to what was originally agreed — useful if scope or pricing shifted slightly along the way.

When you might skip the quotation step

For small, fixed-price, repeat work with an existing client, sending a quotation every time can feel unnecessary. In that case, agreeing on pricing once (even informally) and moving straight to invoicing for each job is common practice — just make sure the agreed rate is documented somewhere both sides can refer back to.
