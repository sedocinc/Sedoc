How to Write a Professional Offer Letter

What goes in it, what to leave out, and the clauses that prevent misunderstandings later.

sedoc Team · Updated September 2026 · Offer Letter Generator

What an offer letter actually needs to do

An offer letter has one job: turn a verbal or informal agreement into a written record both sides can refer back to. It's not the same as an employment contract in every jurisdiction, but it should still be specific enough that neither party is guessing about the terms a few weeks later.

The core clauses every offer letter should include

  • Job title and reporting line — the exact title being offered and who the candidate will report to.
  • Start date — a specific date, not "early next month."
  • Compensation — base salary, pay frequency (monthly, bi-weekly), and currency.
  • Employment type — full-time, part-time, contract, or probationary, and the probation period length if applicable.
  • Work location — on-site, remote, or hybrid, and the specific office if relevant.
  • Benefits summary — leave entitlement, insurance, or other benefits, even briefly.
  • Acceptance deadline — a clear date by which the candidate needs to respond.
  • Conditions — if the offer is contingent on reference checks, background checks, or document verification, state it plainly.

Mistakes that create problems later

The most frequent issue isn't a missing clause — it's vague language. "Competitive salary package" instead of a number. "Standard benefits" instead of a list. "Flexible start date" instead of an actual date. Every vague phrase is a future conversation you'll have to have anyway, except now it happens after the candidate has already resigned from their previous job.

The second common mistake is inconsistency between what was discussed verbally (in an interview or a call) and what's written in the letter. If the numbers or terms differ even slightly, put the corrected, final version in writing and make clear it supersedes any earlier discussion.

Tone matters more than people expect

An offer letter is often a candidate's first real interaction with your company as an employer, not just an interviewer. A letter that's clear, warm, and well-formatted signals the same thing a messy, generic one does — just in the opposite direction. It's worth the extra few minutes to get the formatting and tone right.

This article covers general offer letter structure and is not legal advice. Employment law — including what must legally be included in a written offer — varies by country and sometimes by province or state. Check local labor law or consult an HR/legal professional before finalizing your template.
